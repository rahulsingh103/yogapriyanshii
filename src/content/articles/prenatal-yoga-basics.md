This guide is general information, not medical advice. Every pregnancy is different, so please check with your midwife or doctor before exercising in pregnancy, and follow their advice.

Pregnancy changes how your body feels from one week to the next. Many people find that gentle movement and slow breathing help them feel more at home in a changing body, and prenatal yoga is one calm way to bring both into the week. Health organisations such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and the NHS encourage staying active during an uncomplicated pregnancy, with some sensible adjustments.

## Before you start

- Check with your midwife or doctor before exercising in pregnancy, especially if you have a health condition or any complications.
- Tell your teacher that you are pregnant, and how many weeks along you are, before the class begins.
- Mention anything else that might matter, such as pelvic or back pain, dizziness or a previous injury.
- If you are new to yoga, a class designed for pregnancy is usually a better place to begin than a general class.

## What prenatal yoga usually includes

A prenatal class tends to be slower and softer than a general class. It often includes:

- breathing practices to help you settle and slow down;
- gentle movement for the hips, back and shoulders;
- standing poses for steadiness, often with a wall or chair close by;
- supported positions using cushions, blocks or a bolster;
- time to rest, usually lying on your side or propped up later in pregnancy.

The aim is to feel comfortable and grounded, not to push. As a simple guide, you should still be able to talk while you move.

## Points to keep in mind

Guidance from ACOG and the NHS shares a few common themes:

- Avoid overheating. Practise in a cool, airy room, drink water, and skip hot yoga while you are pregnant.
- Avoid lying flat on your back for long, especially later in pregnancy. Lying on your side, or sitting propped up, is often more comfortable.
- Avoid poses where you could lose your balance and fall. Keep a wall or chair nearby for support, and come out of any pose that feels unsteady.
- Stay within a comfortable range. Your joints can feel looser in pregnancy, so choose easy stretches rather than deep ones.
- Rest whenever you need to. Some days you will have more energy than others, and that is fine.

## When to stop

Stop exercising and contact your midwife, doctor or maternity unit if you notice any warning signs. ACOG lists signs such as:

- bleeding from the vagina, or fluid leaking or gushing;
- regular, painful contractions;
- feeling dizzy or faint;
- chest pain or a headache;
- shortness of breath before you start exercising;
- muscle weakness that affects your balance;
- pain or swelling in your calf.

## After birth

After your baby arrives, give yourself time. Many people return to movement gradually, and it is best to wait until your midwife or doctor is happy for you to begin, often after your postnatal check. Start softly, with breathing and easy movement, and build up at your own pace. There is no rush: your body has done something remarkable.
