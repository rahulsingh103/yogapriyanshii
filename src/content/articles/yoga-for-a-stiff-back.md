This guide is general information, not medical advice. If you are unsure whether yoga is right for you at the moment, speak to a doctor or physiotherapist before you start.

A stiff back is one of the most common reasons people come to yoga. Long hours at a desk, driving, travel and stress can all leave the back feeling tight. Gentle, regular movement is often part of feeling better, and yoga can be a calm way to bring it into your week.

## When to see a doctor or physiotherapist first

See a doctor or physiotherapist if your back pain is severe, sudden or comes with other symptoms. NHS guidance describes signs that need medical help quickly. In plain words, get help first if you have:

- pain that started after a fall, an accident or an injury;
- numbness or tingling, especially around your bottom or genitals;
- new difficulty controlling your bladder or bowels;
- a high temperature, unexplained weight loss or a general feeling of being unwell along with the pain;
- pain that keeps getting worse, is bad at night, or has not improved after a few weeks.

If none of these apply and your back simply feels stiff, gentle yoga may be worth a try.

## What the research says

The research on yoga for low back pain is encouraging, but modest.

- In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guideline on low back pain and sciatica lists group exercise programmes, which can include mind and body approaches, among the options to consider.
- In the US, the American College of Physicians' 2017 guideline lists yoga among the non-drug options for long-term low back pain.
- A 2022 Cochrane review found low-certainty evidence that, compared with no exercise, yoga may bring small improvements in pain and in everyday function for people with long-term low back pain.

In other words, yoga is one reasonable option among several, not a quick fix. It tends to sit best alongside staying generally active.

## Gentle principles

- Start small. A few minutes of easy movement is a good beginning.
- Move slowly and keep breathing. Let the breath set the pace.
- Stay in a comfortable range. A mild stretch is fine; pain is not.
- Choose little and often. Short, regular sessions usually suit a stiff back better than one long, intense one.
- Notice how you feel the next day, and adjust.

## Three gentle movements to try

Use a mat or a carpeted floor, and skip anything that does not feel right today.

- Cat and cow: come onto your hands and knees, hands under your shoulders and knees under your hips. As you breathe in, let your belly soften towards the floor and lift your gaze a little. As you breathe out, gently round your back towards the ceiling and let your head relax. Move slowly for five to eight breaths. Stop if it hurts.
- Knees to chest: lie on your back with your knees bent and your feet on the floor. Bring one knee, or both, towards your chest and hold loosely behind the thighs. Stay for five slow breaths. Stop if it hurts.
- Child's pose: from your hands and knees, sit your hips back towards your heels and rest your forehead on the mat, a folded towel or your stacked hands. Your knees can be wide if that is more comfortable. Stay for five to ten breaths. Stop if it hurts.

## Classes to consider

If you would like guidance in a class, these are gentle places to begin:

- Spine Healing: gentle work for back care and release, at your own pace.
- Mobility Yoga: easy movement for your joints and everyday ease.
- Hatha: steady, clearly explained postures.
- Yin Yoga: long, quiet holds, mostly seated or lying down.
